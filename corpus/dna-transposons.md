# DNA transposons, MITEs and Helitrons

DNA transposons are Class II transposable elements that move as DNA, without an RNA intermediate.

## TIR elements

Most DNA transposons have terminal inverted repeats (TIRs) at both ends and encode a transposase that cuts the element out and pastes it elsewhere. The target site duplication length is characteristic of the superfamily:

- Tc1-Mariner: 2 bp, always TA
- hAT: 8 bp
- Mutator (MULE): 9 to 11 bp
- PIF-Harbinger: 3 bp (TWA)
- CACTA: 2 to 3 bp

The maize Activator/Dissociation (Ac/Ds) system discovered by Barbara McClintock belongs to the hAT superfamily.

## MITEs

Miniature inverted-repeat transposable elements are short (usually under 600 bp), non-autonomous deletion derivatives of DNA transposons. They have TIRs and a TSD but no transposase gene, and they can reach very high copy numbers, often near genes. Stowaway MITEs (Tc1-Mariner related, TA TSD) and Tourist MITEs (PIF-Harbinger related) are common in grass genomes. The rice element mPing is an active MITE.

## Why they are hard to find

TIRs are often only 10 to 50 bp long, so chance inverted repeats are common in a genome and diverged copies lose sequence similarity at their ends quickly. Tools such as TIR-Learner and the TIR module of EDTA combine structural search with homology to known elements.

## Helitrons

Helitrons transpose by a rolling-circle mechanism. They have no TIRs and do not create a TSD; they usually start with TC, end with CTRR, carry a short hairpin near the 3' end and insert between an A and a T. They often capture gene fragments.
