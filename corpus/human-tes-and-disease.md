# Transposable elements in the human genome and disease

About half of the human genome is derived from transposable elements, most of them no longer able to move.

## The active elements

- **LINE-1 (L1)** makes up about 17% of the genome. A full-length element is about 6 kb and encodes ORF1p and ORF2p (endonuclease and reverse transcriptase). Only around 80 to 100 copies per genome are thought to be retrotransposition-competent, mostly of the young L1HS subfamily.
- **Alu** elements are about 300 bp SINEs with over a million copies, roughly 10% of the genome. They have no coding capacity and use the L1 machinery to retrotranspose. AluY is the youngest and most active subfamily.
- **SVA** elements are composite elements of a few kb, also mobilised by L1.

New insertions made by the L1 machinery (target-primed reverse transcription) usually end in a poly(A) tail and are flanked by a target site duplication of variable length, often 7 to 20 bp.

## Disease

New insertions can disrupt exons, alter splicing or cause deletions. More than a hundred disease-causing insertions have been described. Classic examples are L1 insertions in the factor VIII gene causing haemophilia A (Kazazian et al. 1988, Nature) and a de novo Alu insertion in NF1 causing neurofibromatosis type 1 (Wallace et al. 1991, Nature). Somatic L1 insertions are also found in many cancers, especially colorectal and oesophageal tumours.

## Detection

Mobile element insertions are found in short-read whole-genome data from discordant read pairs and split reads at the insertion site, with tools such as MELT, xTea and TEMP. Long reads span whole insertions and make detection and genotyping much easier. Polymorphic insertions differ in frequency between human populations and are used in population genetics.
